# Part 2. One-page summary

**The topic.** A consumer venture in human connection, owned end to end by one person.

**The specific focus.** Two pieces, and they are connected.

One is Nuuko, a journaling app that files what you write by the person it is about instead
of by the date, and the other is the content engine, the software I built to cut, caption
and publish my videos to YouTube, Instagram and TikTok, which I want to release so that
other creators can run it too.

The claim I am testing is that owning the audience and the product at the same time turns
attention into retained users at a rate a normal app cannot buy, and the connection between
the two is not rhetorical: in the 90 days to 21 September 2026 the channel delivered
1,715,696 views and 1,306,099 minutes watched, and gained 5,756 subscribers against 1,238
lost, so those people are the input and the app is what they arrive into. The rate at which
one becomes the other is what I am actually studying, and right now that rate is zero,
because there is no link in either direction.

I checked this framing with Prof Tam and she agreed to it, which matters, because a capstone
that reads as two capstones stapled together is exactly what #cp-curation punishes.

**The approaches.** Software engineering and product design for both halves, applied
statistics for the funnel and the retention analysis, reported with intervals rather than
headline percentages, and user research under HSR for the social layer, building on the
methods already used for Nuuko v1, which were surveys, coded app-store reviews,
semi-structured interviews and thematic analysis, plus an A/B design for person-filing
against the blank-page control that already exists.

**The work product.** One thing with two halves: Nuuko v2, person-filed, with a consented
social layer and tested with more people than the group that has seen it so far, and the
content engine, packaged and documented so that a stranger can clone it and get a finished
video out of a sample take. Each half has its own audience, since Nuuko is for people who
journal about other people and the engine is for creators who want to run it.

**What good success looks like.** Three rubrics, written now rather than afterwards, so I
cannot bend them later to fit whatever happens.

*The app.* Scored 1 to 4 by someone who is not me, on four questions: does a first-time
user finish an entry without being told how, and can they say what the app is for
afterwards; is the privacy claim true as built rather than as described, meaning that every
feature's data path is written down and matches the code; does the social layer make a
private entry harder to leak rather than easier, which means that sharing is a separate
object you create on purpose and never a toggle on something you already wrote; and does it
stand up next to Finch, How We Feel and Day One on the same task?

*The engine.* A 4 is a stranger cloning it, running one command, and getting a finished
video out of a sample take without asking me anything, a 3 needs one fix that is not in the
docs, a 2 runs only on my machine, and a 1 does not run at all. Three to five creators try
it, and I write down where each of them stopped.

*The documentation.* A 4 means a reader can redraw the pipeline's architecture without
opening the code and can name three decisions that were reversed and why, a 3 gets the
architecture but not the reversals, a 2 is a story with no decision record, and a 1 is a
list of what was built.

**The numbers, and where each one starts.** A target with no baseline is a wish, so every
row has a measured starting point.

**The retention number needs saying out loud.** A figure of about 40% has been attached to
Nuuko in my own earlier documents, and it does not have a primary source in any of them,
while the word retention means three different things across those same files, so the number
cannot be compared to anything until its window and its denominator are written down. I am
not carrying it into this brief as a baseline, and establishing it properly is the first
measurement job of the year, which it has to be, because every other row in the table is
read against it. For scale, the median 30-day retention across 93 real-world mental health
apps is 3.3% (Baumel et al., 2019), which is the floor a real number gets judged against and
the reason an unexamined 40% would not survive a second reader.

**What usage actually is today.** 25 registered accounts and 349 entries on the live
database as of 19 September 2026, which is a tester group rather than an audience, and
everyone in it came to the app through me, so every number above is measured on that group
and the year's work includes testing with people who are not part of it.

**What would mean I was wrong.** A funnel that converts no better than ordinary paid
acquisition, or retention that does not improve with the person pivot in place once the
baseline is properly established. I am deliberately not naming a retention threshold here,
because the honest position is that I do not yet have a number to beat, but the threshold
gets written down the moment the baseline exists, and before the pivot ships rather than
after. Either result is a finding, and either one gets written up as one.

**What is in scope, and what is not.** In scope: the YouTube channel and the Instagram
account as the distribution half, the content engine behind them, and Nuuko. Out of scope,
said here so that it stays out: my book and the manuscript behind it, the podcast feed, the

that repository. Some of them share code with the capstone work, but none of them are part
of what I am submitting or what I am measured on, because the channel generates an unlimited

This is the most expensive lesson in my repo, and it has cost me three separate times.
Every scheduled job on my machine stopped for nine days and nothing said so, because macOS
re-stamped the identity owning my log files and launchd opens the log before it starts the
program, so the thing that failed was the logging itself: exit 78, no output, no error
anywhere. Twelve videos went up from the wrong folder and every check passed, because each
check compared the upload against the file it had been wrongly copied from. And a tool I
built to show me what a viewer sees drew YouTube's duration badge at a quarter of its real
size, which made it a check that shrank the obstacle rather than measuring it.

Scored on four things: does each automated stage check its own output rather than its exit
code; where a check models something in the world, is that model measured against the real
thing rather than guessed; was the check written before or after a fault shipped; and did a
fault that did ship produce a new check, or only a fix.

### \#voicefidelity

Measuring one person's writing as a distribution and holding machine-assisted text to it,
instead of trying to detect machine writing.

I built the detector first and it turned out to be the wrong question, because my most
conversational published pages scored the most machine-like and any threshold tuned to catch
generated prose flagged my own writing. So the second system asks whether text is mine
rather than whether it is a machine's: the target is my measured habits, the corpus is
72,145 words of my own published pages, and new text gets compared against that, with the
system reporting where it sits as a distance rather than as a verdict.

It found a real and specific gap, which is that my own prose runs contractions at 28,969 per
million while the assisted prose ran 2,232, thirteen times fewer, with no fragments and no
questions in it at all. That is a measurable difference, and it is exactly the kind a
pass-or-fail detector cannot report.

This one matters for the capstone beyond the app, because I write with AI and the handbook
asks about that directly, and a system that can say how far a draft has drifted from how I
actually write is a better answer than a promise that I edited it.

Scored on four things: is the corpus big enough and clean enough for the statistic being
claimed; does the system report a distance from a measured target rather than a pass or fail
against a threshold somebody chose; is it run on real drafts rather than on test cases; and
has it changed a piece of writing that would otherwise have shipped.

### \#autonomyboundary

Deciding, for an automated system, where the automation stops and a person has to look. And
writing that boundary down as part of the system rather than keeping it as a habit.

I run 30 scheduled jobs that post to Instagram, TikTok and YouTube, and every one of them
has a written line saying what it may not do, with the fleet split in two on exactly this
question: jobs that make no judgements run on a server and need no laptop, while jobs that
need judgement run on my machine. Nothing sends an email, it only drafts one, nothing pushes
to the app's main branch, and nothing switches on a payment. The Shorts gate is the sharpest
case, because it exits non-zero rather than printing advice, since a note in a file is only
advice to whoever reads it and nobody was reading it, which is how 108 Shorts went out
against a budget of 20.

Scored on four things: is the boundary written down where the system is rather than
remembered; is each stop justified by a consequence rather than by caution; is it enforced
by something that fails rather than by something that warns; and when it was wrong, was it
moved on purpose and dated rather than worked around without saying so.

### \#guaranteepreservation

Adding a capability to a system without weakening a guarantee it already makes, and proving
the guarantee still holds afterwards.

Nuuko retains people partly because nothing leaves the device and there is no sign-up wall,
and the research says privacy failures are why people leave the competitors, so a social
layer, which wants accounts, a server, sync and moderation, would destroy the finding the
app was built on if it were built carelessly. The design keeps both: the private journal is
untouched, anything shared is a separate object you create on purpose rather than a setting
on something you already wrote, and it is encrypted on the device to a key that travels only
inside the invite link, so the server holds ciphertext it cannot read.

Two real faults on the way show why this needs to be assessed rather than assumed, since
sync used to send an entry as plaintext if encryption threw, and the coach was pulling
encrypted rows out of the database and sending them to Google. A third is a wording fault
rather than a code one: my mini capstone says the ML layer runs entirely on the device with
no external calls, which is true of the classifier but not of the coach.

Scored on four things: is the existing guarantee stated precisely enough to be violated;
does the new capability sit beside it rather than on top of it, so that the old path is
unchanged; is the guarantee demonstrated against the code as built rather than as described;
and are the places where it does not hold named out loud rather than left out.

### Two I considered and am not proposing

Instrumenting content decisions against my own preference is already the worked example under
